# Sb2Se3 switching speed: does "100 ns, non-volatile" survive contact with the literature?

**Verdict (revised): the binding constraint is row-serial addressing, not raw switching speed, and not primarily power either.** Our actual write mechanism (DRAFT.md §8.10: a 2T1R sub-pixel — select transistor, heater-driver transistor, resistive TiN heater — "identical to Intel Optane PCM memory," addressed row-at-a-time over 2 shared wires) is electrically switched; the free-space laser is readout illumination only and never writes a pixel. Our closest architectural match in the measured literature is Fang et al. 2023 (arXiv:2307.12103): free-space transmissive metasurface, electrically switched by a doped-Si microheater, multi-level phase — feature-for-feature the same concept as our design. On that measured device (SET/crystallization = 50 µs), raw switching speed is genuinely *not* the blocker: 333 time-multiplexed subframes fit inside a 60 Hz frame on switching speed alone, beating Choi et al.'s existing hardware. But DRAFT.md's own specified addressing scheme — row-at-a-time over 2 shared wires, exactly like the crossbar/2T1R topology of real PCM memory — means only 338 pixels (one row) can be driven at once, and a full hogel/panel update needs 338 such rows in strict sequence. At Fang's measured SET time that is **16.9 ms per full 8-level update — already longer than one 60 Hz frame period (16.7 ms), before any multiplexing is even attempted.** Power is a real, independent second wall (fully using the 333-subframe switching-speed headroom would cost ~11.4 kW, absurd for a headset), but it is not the first wall hit: the addressing topology fails first, and it would fail even if switching were instantaneous. DRAFT.md's own "100 ns pulse, 29,586× margin per row" line is also wrong, but it is a **costed, non-fatal error**: the real measured SET time is 500× slower than the assumed 100 ns, so the real margin is 29,586 / 500 ≈ **59×** — still positive, at the row level. What kills the multiplexing plan is not that margin going away, but that the *rows do not run in parallel*, so the per-row margin never accumulates into frame-level headroom. An earlier version of this document used Lawson et al. (optical, free-space-*written*) as "the scenario matching our design"; that was a geometry misclassification — free-space describes how we *read* the panel, not how we *write* it — and it is corrected below. Lawson is retained only as a counterfactual for what optical writing would have cost us.

**Consistency checks that increase confidence in these numbers:** Lawson's crystallization temperature (473 K) matches Ríos's independently-measured 200°C (=473 K) to the kelvin, despite completely different device geometries and actuation methods. Every single Sb₂Se₃ row — nine devices, five groups, both optical and electrical stimulus — has SET slower than RESET; the direction of the asymmetry is not in question anywhere in the literature we found. What varies by 5 orders of magnitude across rows is the *size* of the asymmetry, and that size tracks geometry, not material identity.

## Geometry dependence: how much of this is the material, how much is the device — and which geometry are we?

**We are an electrically-switched device.** DRAFT.md §8.10 is explicit: 2T1R per sub-pixel (select transistor + heater-driver transistor + resistive TiN heater), "identical to Intel Optane PCM memory," with a process flow of "Standard 28nm CMOS → DBR deposition → TiN heater patterning → Sb₂Se₃ film → MgF₂ AR coating." The TiN heater sits in direct contact with every sub-pixel; the free-space laser illuminates the panel for holographic *readout*, not to write phase state. This means Fang et al.'s free-space, electrically-switched metasurface (arXiv:2307.12103) — not Lawson's optically-*written* thin film — is our architectural family: free-space optics for the wavefront, electrical Joule heating for the phase state.

The asymmetry factor (SET time ÷ RESET time) by geometry:

- **Free-space, electrically switched, our own write mechanism (Fang et al.):** ~40
- **Waveguide, electrically switched, best measured (Yu et al.):** ~30
- **Waveguide, electrically switched, fastest RESET (Yang et al.):** ~148
- **GST, waveguide, electrically switched (Zhou et al.):** ~4 (far more symmetric)
- **Free-space, optically switched (Lawson — counterfactual, not our device):** ~7.5×10⁶

The pattern: **every electrically-switched, heat-sunk Sb₂Se₃ geometry we found — waveguide or free-space metasurface alike — compresses the SET/RESET asymmetry to a 30–150× range (under 2.2 orders of magnitude), and the one electrically-switched GST device compresses it further still, to ~4×.** The dominant variable is not "free-space vs. waveguide," it is "electrical Joule heating with a nearby heat sink vs. optical pumping through a low-conductivity cap": RESET (amorphization) is a melt-and-quench process whose speed is set by how fast heat leaves the excited volume, and a TiN or doped-Si heater in direct contact with the PCM does that far better than an external laser pulse absorbed through a dielectric cap. SET (crystallization) is nucleation- and growth-limited in every geometry — Lawson's own transient measurement (their Fig. 5a) shows crystal growth onset delayed by ~300 ms regardless of eventual target fraction, an incubation delay that a shorter or gentler pulse cannot shortcut — which is why SET stays the slow direction everywhere, but a well-engineered electrical heater still reaches useful SET times (20–600 µs) that a free-space optical write, going through Lawson's low-conductivity ZnS:SiO₂ cap, does not (~300 ms–1 s).

**What this implies for our 300 nm film on a 0.5 µm pixel specifically:** our write mechanism (TiN heater in direct contact with the PCM, per DRAFT §8.10) puts us in Fang's family, not Lawson's — this is the correction from the previous version of this document. Fang's device uses a 20 nm Sb₂Se₃ film; ours is 15× thicker (300 nm), which raises thermal mass and, for a diffusion-limited quench, pushes the RESET time up roughly with the square of thickness — so our real RESET is plausibly slower than Fang's measured 600 ns–1.25 µs. SET's incubation-dominated ~50 µs is less obviously thickness-sensitive (nucleation onset is not primarily a bulk-diffusion process the way quenching is), so Fang's 50 µs SET is treated as the best available direct estimate for our design, while Fang's RESET number is treated as optimistic. **We use Fang's numbers as the primary, best-available-match estimate, Yu et al.'s wafer-scale electrical device as a deliberately more conservative bound, and Lawson purely to quantify the cost we avoid by not writing optically.**

## What this means for time multiplexing

Numbers below are computed by `tier1_tmm/pcm_switching_budget.py`; see that file for the full derivation and `tests/test_pcm_switching.py` for the sympy-verified closed forms behind them.

**1. Frame rate for a full 8-level update, assuming (wrongly, see point 3) that the whole array can be addressed in parallel.** For a parallel-addressed panel, the frame cannot be presented until every pixel that needs to change has finished switching — so the frame time is bounded by the *slowest* pixel required that frame, not the average one. Working out the probability that *not one* of our 30 Gpixel panel's pixels needs the slow (SET) direction this frame — `(1 - 7/16)^(3.0e10)` — gives a number below `1e-300`: for any array bigger than a few dozen pixels, "worst case" and "average case" are the same number, not two different design points.

| Scenario | max frame rate (Hz), fully-parallel assumption |
|---|---|
| **Fang et al. — PRIMARY** | **20,000** |
| Yu et al. — pessimistic bound | 1,667 |
| Lawson — optical counterfactual (not our design) | 3.3 |

Under this (incorrect, see point 3) fully-parallel assumption, our primary scenario looks *excellent* — far better than the pessimistic bound, and dramatically better than the optical counterfactual (3.3 Hz, which really would be fatal for a real device, and is exactly what our electrical write mechanism avoids).

**2. Subframes per 60 Hz / 90 Hz frame under the same fully-parallel assumption, and the PSNR it buys (Choi et al., arXiv:2205.02367).**

| Scenario | Subframes @60Hz | PSNR (dB) | SSIM | Subframes @90Hz | PSNR (dB) | SSIM |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fang et al. — PRIMARY** | **333** | 30.95 (extrapolated) | 0.982 (extrapolated) | 222 | 30.07 (extrapolated) | 0.959 (extrapolated) |
| Yu et al. — pessimistic bound | 27 | 25.49 | 0.839 | 18 | 24.61 | 0.816 |
| Lawson — optical counterfactual | 0 | 18.33 (floor) | 0.652 (floor) | 0 | 18.33 (floor) | 0.652 (floor) |

On switching speed alone, our primary scenario beats Choi et al.'s own TI DLP6750 hardware (which runs subframes at 694 µs) by ~14×, and comfortably clears the pessimistic bound too. **If the addressing bandwidth existed to use it, switching speed would not be the reason to worry about this architecture.** It doesn't exist — see point 3.

**3. Row-serial addressing: the constraint that actually binds, discovered by taking DRAFT.md §8.10 literally.** DRAFT states addressing is "Row-at-a-time (DRAM-style)" over "2 wires (row+col), 5 tracks available at 28nm" — the same crossbar/2T1R topology real PCM memory uses, and for the same reason: with only a shared row-select and column-drive line, exactly one physical row (338 pixels, driven in parallel by independent column drivers) can be active at a time. A full hogel — and, under hogel-level parallel scanning across the panel (see below), the whole panel — needs 338 such rows in strict sequence:

`hogel_row_serial_time = 338 × t_set`

| Scenario | t_set | full-array update time | equivalent Hz |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fang et al. — PRIMARY** | 50 µs | **16.90 ms** | **59.2** |
| Yu et al. — pessimistic bound | 600 µs | 202.8 ms | 4.9 |
| Lawson — optical counterfactual | 300 ms | 101.4 s | 0.01 |

**Even our best-matched, primary, electrically-switched scenario cannot complete one un-multiplexed 8-level update inside a 60 Hz frame (16.90 ms > 16.67 ms budget) — a shortfall of only ~1.4%, but a shortfall.** Time-multiplexing needs *multiple* full passes inside one frame; row-serial addressing does not leave room for even one with full margin, let alone several, regardless of how fast the underlying material switches. This is a **tighter, more binding constraint than either raw switching speed (point 2, which by itself looked fine) or switching energy (point 4, below)** — it would matter even if the material switched instantaneously, because it is a wiring/topology limit, not a materials limit.

*Is hogel-level parallel scanning a reasonable assumption, or wishful thinking?* It is not an assumption this analysis introduces — it is the only reading under which DRAFT's own published numbers are self-consistent. DRAFT states the refresh target is **1 FPS** (DRAFT.md line 19: "Low refresh rate chosen to eliminate computational/bandwidth bottlenecks and allow non-traditional slow-switching materials") and separately states "2.96 ms/row." `1 s / 338 rows = 2.9586 ms`, reproducing DRAFT's own number to 3 significant figures — but that arithmetic only equals a **panel-wide** 1 FPS if every one of the 512×512 hogels scans its own 338 rows in parallel with every other hogel. The alternative (one shared row-scanner for the entire panel, 512×338 = 173,056 total physical rows) gives 173,056 × 2.96 ms ≈ 512 seconds per frame, not 1 second — flatly contradicting DRAFT's own "1 FPS" line. So hogel-level parallelism is required for DRAFT's own numbers to hold together, even though DRAFT never states it explicitly. Under the pessimistic, no-hogel-parallelism reading, the numbers are far worse: 0.12 Hz (Fang), 0.01 Hz (Yu), 0.00002 Hz (Lawson) for the whole panel.

*Is the row-serial bound actually a current-delivery limit — could more than one row be driven at once if there were enough current?* No. Scaling Fang et al.'s measured per-pixel drive current (their 30 µm × 30 µm pixel: 4.1 mA SET, 10 mA RESET) down to our 0.5 µm sub-pixel by area (a rough estimate — no device at our pixel scale exists to measure this directly) gives ~1.1 µA (SET) and ~2.8 µA (RESET) per pixel, or ~0.4–0.9 mA for a full 338-pixel row driven in parallel — trivially within normal CMOS driver capability. **Row-serial addressing is a wiring/topology choice (DRAFT's own "2 wires, 5 tracks"), not a current-capacity limit**, and could in principle be relaxed by adding more independent row/column drivers — but that competes directly with the already-tight per-pixel routing budget DRAFT itself flags as barely sufficient at 0.5 µm pitch. If every hogel really does scan in lockstep, the *aggregate* panel current (100–250 A across 262,144 hogels) is a real power-delivery-network engineering problem in its own right — comparable to a high-end GPU's supply current, solvable but not free, and separate from the switching-*energy* budget below.

**4. Does non-volatility mean a pixel skips switching when it isn't changing? Yes for energy, no for timing.** Two regimes need to be told apart:

- *Intra-frame time-multiplexed dither subframes* (Choi et al.'s scheme: several distinct SLM patterns per displayed frame, each a different quantization-noise realization, averaged by the eye/sensor for speckle reduction) are near-independent draws *by design* — decorrelation between subframes is the entire point of dithering. Under a uniform-random model, the fraction of pixels whose target level changes between consecutive dither subframes is `1 - 1/8 = 87.5%`. Non-volatility saves essentially nothing here on *timing* (point 3's row-serial bound gates the array regardless of how many pixels change), but it does cut *energy* roughly in proportion to the ~12.5% of pixels that hold their state.
- *Frame-to-frame video content updates* (the outer refresh, e.g. a moving 3D scene) are a genuinely different regime with real temporal redundancy. We do not have an arXiv-measured number for *our* content's redundancy, so this is stated as an assumption, swept for sensitivity: at an assumed 5–30% of pixels changing per video frame, 1.5–9.0 Gpixels out of the 30 Gpixel panel need to switch each frame.

**5. Energy.** Using a first-principles thermal-minimum estimate (heat a 0.5 µm × 0.5 µm × 300 nm Sb₂Se₃ pixel from 300 K to its crystallization temperature 473 K for SET, or its melting point 884 K for RESET, both measured/cross-validated values from Lawson and Ríos; excludes latent heat, so this is a lower bound): E_set ≈ 11.4 pJ, E_reset ≈ 38.4 pJ per pixel. No device at this pixel scale has been built (per `docs/research_holo_sota.md` §6), so there is no measured energy number to use instead.

- **60 Hz video-only, 15% of pixels changing, no multiplexing: ~5.9 W.** Plausible for a headset-scale optical engine, in every scenario.
- **Fully multiplexed at Fang's primary-scenario switching speed (333 subframes/frame, 87.5% redraw each): ~11.4 kW.**
- **Fully multiplexed at Yu's pessimistic-bound switching speed (27 subframes/frame): ~924 W.**

**The multiplexed-power numbers are absurd and are flagged as such — but they are theoretical, not the reason the multiplexing plan fails first.** Point 3 already shows the panel cannot attempt anywhere near 27 or 333 subframes per frame given row-serial addressing (it cannot even complete *one* full pass at 60 Hz in the primary scenario). Power is a real, independent second wall: even *if* the addressing bottleneck were solved — more wiring tracks, hogel-bank parallelism, whatever it takes — fully exploiting the resulting switching-speed headroom would still not be thermally survivable. The multiplexing plan fails twice over, for two unrelated reasons, and the addressing bottleneck fails first.

## Escape routes

The addressing bottleneck is the news that matters most here, so these routes are ordered around it. "Switch to electrical (Joule-heating) addressing" is *not* listed — DRAFT.md §8.10 already specifies exactly that (2T1R, TiN heater); it is not an available additional lever, it is the baseline this whole analysis is built on, and it is precisely what keeps switching speed itself from being fatal (point 2).

1. **Break the row-serial bottleneck with hogel-bank parallelism, not per-pixel rewiring.** The tight "2 wires, 5 tracks" budget applies *inside* a hogel; it says nothing about how many hogels can have independent row/column driver periphery. Real large-scale arrays (DRAM banks, column-parallel image sensor readout, multi-block display drivers) routinely parallelize at exactly this coarser grain without touching the per-pixel wiring budget. This analysis already assumes hogel-level parallelism (it is required for DRAFT's own "1 FPS" number to make sense) — the open question is whether *finer-than-hogel* banking (e.g. quarter-hogel blocks, each with its own row scanner) is achievable, which would proportionally cut the 16.9 ms per-hogel figure. This is the most promising lever because it targets the constraint that actually binds.
2. **Relax the routing budget, if the process node allows it.** DRAFT's 2-wire/5-track figure is specific to 28 nm CMOS at 0.5 µm pitch. A process with more available metal tracks, or a 3D-integrated (heater layer separate from the routing layer) approach, could allow more than one row's worth of independent drive at a time. This directly trades off against the "no fabrication precedent at 150 PPI, visible band" gap already flagged in `docs/research_holo_sota.md` §6 — it is a real engineering lift, not a paper change.
3. **Engineer the power-delivery network for the aggregate lockstep current (100–250 A), separately from the addressing fix.** Even with hogel-parallel scanning solved, driving every hogel simultaneously draws GPU-scale aggregate current. This is solvable (large chips do it) but must be designed for explicitly, and interacts with the switching-*energy* budget (point 5) once any multiplexing beyond a single pass is attempted.
4. **Consider GST or GSST instead of Sb₂Se₃, now a smaller lever than it looked before.** The GST rows in our table show asymmetry factors of ~4×, versus ~30–150× for electrically-switched Sb₂Se₃ — a real advantage, but since the row-serial addressing bottleneck binds regardless of material switching speed, a faster or more symmetric material narrows the (already survivable) point-2 headroom without touching the point-3 bottleneck that actually matters. Worth it primarily if GST's higher optical loss (why `docs/research_holo_sota.md` §3 currently prefers Sb₂Se₃/Sb₂S₃) can be separately resolved.
5. **Give up on the assumption that every subframe needs a fresh 8-level redraw.** If future hologram-optimization work can favor *low-transition* dither sequences, the 87.5% redraw fraction used above is a design choice, not a law of nature — but per point 3, this reduces energy (point 5), not the row-serial timing bound, since even one changed pixel per row still gates that row at t_set.
