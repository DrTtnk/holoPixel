## 1. Shi, Li, Kim, Kellnhofer & Matusik, "Towards real-time photorealistic 3D holography with deep neural networks," *Nature* 591, 234–239 (2021), DOI:10.1038/s41586-020-03152-0

### Verdict

This is a real, fully-detailed Nature article (not a short communication) with a complete Methods section and 10 Extended Data figures, and every number our earlier survey could only cite second-hand is confirmed in the text. The headline "60 Hz at 1920×1080 on a consumer GPU" claim is real but belongs to the *smallest* of three published network variants ("CNN mini", 8 conv layers); the full-capacity network the authors call "our model" (30 layers, 24 filters/layer) — the one that reaches ≈0.97 SSIM — runs at only 25 Hz under the same measurement. The network is trained at a fixed 384×384 (or 192×192) resolution but is genuinely fully convolutional with no pooling or dense layers, so it can be run at other resolutions (this is exactly how the 1920×1080 numbers were obtained) — but that flexibility is a uniform, whole-frame resize, not a spatially-varying compute graph. There is no mechanism in this architecture, at any capacity point the authors publish, that spends more computation in one image region than another. It is a strong existence proof that a learned CGH network can be architecture-flexible in output size, and a clear demonstration that it is *not* natively foveation-capable.

### Architecture, receptive field, parameters

The network is a plain residual-block stack: an initial 3×3 conv, a chain of identical residual blocks (each block = two 3×3 conv + batch-renorm + ReLU with a residual add), a final 3×3 conv + batch-renorm + ReLU, and one long skip connection that concatenates the raw RGB-D input onto the feature map feeding the penultimate residual block (Fig. 1c). There is no pooling and no transposed convolution anywhere — the authors state explicitly that avoiding these (unlike U-Net or Dilated-Net) is why their architecture wins the quality-at-equal-runtime comparison, because contraction/expansion operators disrupt the Fresnel-zone-plate-like high-frequency patterns the network needs to reproduce (Methods, "CNN model architecture," p.236). Depth is set by matching the aggregate receptive field to the width of the *maximum subhologram* (the Fresnel zone plate footprint), and the paper derives a closed-form minimum-layer count from that geometric requirement (Eq. — unlabeled, p.236: minimally required layers = [(W−3)/2] + 1). A "midpoint hologram" pre-processing trick (propagating the target hologram to the centre of an unmagnified frustum, Eq. 2, Extended Data Fig. 3) collapses the required subhologram width from hundreds of pixels down to as little as 30 px in their rendering configuration, which is what makes a 15-layer minimum (and a practical 30-layer default) feasible at all — without it, W could require hundreds of layers.

No absolute parameter count is stated. We back it out from the stated memory footprint (617 KB at FP32) as ≈158,000 parameters; treat this as our estimate, not a number Shi et al. give directly.

### Runtime, precisely separated by hardware class

The abstract's "60 Hz... on a single consumer-grade graphics processing unit" is easy to misread as the full-quality network's number. It is not: Fig. 2d plots three CNN capacity points side by side with runtime labels 25 Hz, 40 Hz, 60 Hz for the 30-layer default, 15-layer "small," and 8-layer "mini" models respectively, all measured on the same NVIDIA Titan RTX with TensorRT. The default model — the one whose ≈0.97 SSIM numbers are quoted throughout the paper as "our model" — runs at 25 Hz, not 60 Hz. The 60 Hz headline belongs to the mini model, whose SSIM (≈0.925, Fig. 2d) is described by the authors themselves as only "gracefully degraded." All of these are measured, not simulated, GPU numbers. The mobile figure (iPhone 11 Pro, A13 Bionic neural engine, 1.1 Hz) and the edge figure (Google Edge TPU, Int8, 2.0 Hz) are both measured on physical devices as well (Methods author-contributions note explicitly credits an iPhone-11-Pro demo and an Edge TPU demo as measured work, p.238–239). No number in this paper is simulation-only; the OA-PBM/PBM comparison numbers used as the speed baseline in Fig. 2d were likewise measured on GPU (with an explicit note that the WRP baseline was accelerated fairly for the comparison, p.236 Methods).

### Training dataset (MIT-CGH-4K)

Built with a custom NVIDIA OptiX ray tracer plus the AI-accelerated denoiser. Each of the 4,000 scenes randomly places 200–250 meshes (sampled with repetition from a pool of >50 meshes) with randomly assigned textures from a pool of >60,000 texture images, with mesh depth drawn from a derived closed-form PDF (Eqs. 1, 9–12) chosen so the *resulting pixel depth distribution is statistically uniform* across the view frustum (0.15 m to 10 m) — this is explicitly contrasted with the DeepFocus dataset, whose depth histogram is heavily front/rear-biased (Extended Data Fig. 2c). Ground-truth holograms for each scene are computed by the paper's own occlusion-aware point-based method (OA-PBM, described below), rendered at three wavelengths (450/520/638 nm) matching the physical prototype's laser, at two resolutions (384×384 for an assumed 8 µm SLM pitch, 192×192 for 16 µm). Training used 3,800 of the 4,000 scenes, with 100 held out for test and 100 for validation; 1,000 epochs, batch size 2, Adam at a constant 1×10⁻⁴ learning rate, on a single Tesla V100 for 84 hours (Methods, p.236–237).

### Image quality numbers, and against what baselines

All numeric SSIM/PSNR figures in the paper are relative comparisons among methods evaluated on the same held-out 100-scene MIT-CGH-4K test partition (Methods states the 3,800/100/100 split; Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 4a/4b use "amplitude SSIM/PSNR" and "focal stack SSIM/PSNR" as the two metrics throughout — no LPIPS, no HDR-VDP anywhere in this paper). Three separate comparisons exist and should not be conflated:

1. **Speed/quality trade-off across CNN capacity** (Fig. 2d): OA-PBM (ground truth, trivial SSIM=1) vs. PBM (no occlusion; ≈0.85 SSIM, clearly the worst, due to background leaking into the foreground) vs. the 30/15/8-layer CNN variants (≈0.97 / ≈0.955 / ≈0.925 SSIM at 25/40/60 Hz respectively).
2. **Architecture comparison at matched runtime** (Extended Data Fig. 4a): the paper's own architecture vs. U-Net vs. Dilated-Net, all reconfigured to the same inference time. Their model wins on both SSIM and PSNR (≈0.97 vs ≈0.96 vs ≈0.95 SSIM; ≈35 vs ≈34 vs ≈33 dB PSNR).
3. **Miniaturization-strategy comparison at matched runtime** (Extended Data Fig. 4b): reducing layer count beats increasing interleave rate, which beats reducing filter count, for the same wall-clock budget.
4. **Loss-function ablation** (Fig. 2f): the full loss (data-fidelity + attention-weighted perceptual/focal-stack loss, β=0.35) outperforms three ablations — no attention mask (β=0), perceptual loss only (no data term, which degrades generalization badly), and data loss only (no perceptual term, which degrades focal-stack/perceptual quality specifically).

Qualitative-only evaluations (no numeric SSIM given) appear for two standard resolution test charts (USAF-1951, RCA Indian-head, Extended Data Fig. 4c), two additional computer-rendered scenes (Extended Data Fig. 5), and two real-world captured scenes from the ETH light-field dataset (Extended Data Fig. 6) — all shown only as visual focal-stack comparisons, and all also photographed through the physical prototype (Extended Data Figs. 9, 10; Fig. 3).

### Occlusion and per-pixel depth handling

Ground-truth occlusion is handled entirely at the *dataset generation* stage, not inside the CNN's forward pass at inference time. The OA-PBM reconstructs a triangulated surface mesh from each RGB-D image and performs a ray-triangle intersection test from every object point to every SLM pixel; wavefront contributions from points whose ray is blocked are multiplied by a binary visibility mask v_o(p) (Eq. 7) before summation into the target hologram (Eq. 8). The CNN itself never runs an explicit visibility test — the authors argue (main text, p.235–236) that the network's ReLU nonlinearities implicitly learn to "selectively distribute intermediate results" in a way that approximates stopping the propagation of occluded wavefronts, purely because it is trained to reproduce OA-PBM-generated ground truth that already has correct occlusion baked in. Per-pixel focal control comes from the same RGB-D depth channel: the loss function's dynamic focal stack (Eq. 4) reconstructs the predicted vs. ground-truth hologram at depth planes sampled per training iteration (some fixed at the dominant depths in the current image, some randomly sampled elsewhere, Methods p.237), which is what teaches the network to focus each pixel correctly rather than just match a single 2D projection.

### Stated failure modes and limitations

The Discussion (p.239) is explicit about two limitations: (1) an RGB-D image is *not* a complete 3D scene description — it has no information about occluded or out-of-frustum geometry, so holograms from this pipeline are not well suited to viewing under large-baseline motion parallax by an untracked or multi-person viewer (the authors argue this is acceptable for eye-tracked HMDs where the hologram is recomputed on demand at the current viewpoint, but flag it as a real constraint, not a solved problem); and (2) the pipeline learns a complex-valued hologram and only converts to phase-only afterward via a separate deterministic encoding step (AA-DPM) — the authors state that "tensor holography itself can be further improved by directly learning phase-only holograms, avoiding explicit complex-to-phase-only conversion," i.e., they flag this as unaddressed future work, not something already solved. A further, less prominent limitation appears in Methods (p.235–236): OA-PBM (their own training-data generator) is itself only a first-order approximation of Fresnel diffraction — genuinely modelling wavefronts from secondary point sources at occlusion boundaries (a Huygens-Fresnel-based improvement) is described as "theoretically possible" but computationally "intractable for complex scenes," so the CNN's quality ceiling is bounded by the physical fidelity of its (approximate) teacher, not by full wave optics.

### Is the compute graph fixed-resolution? (the foveation question)

No, not in the sense of "architecturally locked to one input/output size" — but also not in the sense that matters for foveation. The network is fully convolutional with no pooling and no fully-connected layers, so nothing in its structure depends on a specific image size; the Methods section states this explicitly: "as the CNN is fully convolutional, as long as the pixel pitch remains the same, the trained model can be used to infer RGB-D inputs of arbitrary spatial resolution at test time" (p.236). This is exactly how the headline 1,920×1,080 runtime numbers were obtained from a network trained on 384×384 crops — it is a real, demonstrated capability, not a claim we are extrapolating.

However, this resolution flexibility is *global and uniform*: the same fixed stack of learned 3×3 kernels (with a computed, fixed ≈61-pixel receptive field for the default model) is convolved identically over every pixel of the frame, regardless of position. There is no gating, no input-dependent branching, no early-exit, and no mechanism anywhere in the paper — not even hinted at as future work — by which the network could apply a smaller receptive field, fewer channels, or fewer layers to some spatial region (e.g., the periphery of a foveated HMD render) than another, within a single forward pass. Changing the *whole frame's* resolution changes cost roughly linearly with pixel count (which is why the mini/small/default models trade global resolution/quality for global speed), but nothing in the architecture varies compute density *within* one frame. Concretely: this network could be evaluated at native resolution in a small foveal crop and at reduced resolution elsewhere (two separate forward passes on two differently sized RGB-D crops, then composited) — but that is a system-level foveation scheme built *around* tensor holography, not anything tensor holography itself does, and the paper neither describes nor evaluates it.

### Phase quantization / SLM bit depth

The paper's own prototype quantizes each hologram to 8 bits to match the bit depth of its physical SLM (HOLOEYE PLUTO-2-VIS-014, 1,920×1,080, 8 µm pitch, LCoS reflective phase-only) — stated once, in the "Holographic display prototype" Methods paragraph (p.238), with no further discussion. There is no treatment anywhere in the paper of low-bit-depth phase quantization (3-bit, 4-bit, or otherwise), no ablation of quantization level vs. quality, and no discussion of non-uniform or resonance-driven phase-level spacing. This paper is not a source of evidence, positive or negative, for HoloPixel's 3-bit/8-level modulator design; it simply assumes a conventional high-bit-depth LC-SLM throughout.

### What this changes for HoloPixel

- The 60 Hz / 1,920×1,080 / consumer-GPU number that gets cited as "tensor holography's headline speed" belongs to the lowest-quality (8-layer, ≈0.925 SSIM) variant, not the ≈0.97-SSIM model. Any comparison of HoloPixel's throughput (17.2 Gpixel/12.6 s ≈ 1.36 Gpixel/s on an RTX 5090) against "tensor holography's speed" should specify which of the three published capacity points is the comparison target, since they span 25–60 Hz at fixed 1,920×1,080×3-colour output (roughly 31–75 Mpixel/s at that resolution) — two to three orders of magnitude below HoloPixel's current hogel-grid throughput, but at a completely different task (single 2D RGB-D input, single flat SLM plane, no hogel/hemisphere sampling at all).
- Confirms, from primary source, that a fully convolutional architecture is a viable way to make a CGH network resolution-flexible — this is a legitimate design pattern to consider if HoloPixel ever wants a single trained model that serves multiple hogel-grid resolutions. But it also confirms the negative half of that finding directly relevant to our foveation questions: resolution-flexible is not the same as foveation-capable. Getting real per-region compute savings would require a genuinely new mechanism (region-conditional network depth/width, early-exit, or a tiled multi-model system), not just "run the existing network at a smaller size."
- The training-time cost (84 GPU-hours on one V100, plus a bespoke 4,000-scene ray-traced dataset with a hand-derived depth-uniformizing PDF) is a real data point for how expensive it would be to train an analogous learned model for a hogel/hemisphere representation instead of a single flat RGB-D image — the input/output tensor size for a hogel-based tensor-holography analogue would be far larger (512×512 hogels × 256×256 hemisphere vs. this paper's single 384×384 or 1,920×1,080 frame), so both the dataset generation cost and the training wall-clock should be expected to scale up substantially, not stay comparable.
- The OA-PBM's per-training-sample ground-truth generation (occlusion-aware ray-traced Fresnel simulation) is a candidate reference implementation if HoloPixel ever wants a physically-grounded ground-truth generator for its own future learned surrogate — but note it is explicitly a first-order (non-secondary-diffraction) approximation, so it inherits the same accuracy ceiling our own scatter/occlusion step would need to be checked against.

## 2. Akşit, Kavaklı, Walton, Steed, Urey, Kuffner Dos Anjos, Friston, Weyrich & Ritschel, "Perceptually guided Computer-Generated Holography," Proc. SPIE 12026, Photonics West / Adv. Display Tech. XII, DOI:10.1117/12.2610251 (2022)

### Verdict

This is a 4-page SPIE proceedings paper, and reading it in full confirms — even more strongly than our earlier second-hand summary suggested — that it is purely a conference-format restatement of the TOG/arXiv paper Metameric Varifocal Holography (Walton et al., arXiv:2110.01981, cited here as reference [14], "our main publication"). The paper states outright, in its own §2, "we introduce technical details of our CGH pipeline in our main publication," and then gives only prose descriptions of the three contributions (metameric loss, metameric varifocal hologram pipeline, prototype) with zero equations, zero tables, and a single simulated (not measured) qualitative figure. There are no wall-clock numbers, no iteration counts, no FLOP counts, no loss-function formula, and no definition of eccentricity-dependent pooling regions anywhere in this document — all of that content, to the extent it exists at all, lives only in arXiv:2110.01981, not here. On the specific question of whether foveating a CGH pipeline has ever been shown to save compute: this paper contains no compute numbers of any kind, so it cannot and does not break that negative finding — it neither confirms nor contradicts it; it simply says nothing quantitative on the subject at all.

### Is it only a restatement, or does it add anything?

It is only a restatement, and the paper says so about itself. The three "contributions" listed in §2 — (1) a metameric loss function, (2) a metameric varifocal hologram optimization pipeline, (3) a proof-of-concept prototype — map one-to-one onto the abstract and system description of arXiv:2110.01981 as summarized in our own foveated-holography survey (which already reports 2110.01981's wall-clock numbers: 90 s cold-start, ~4 s warm-started, Sec. 3.1 of that paper). This SPIE paper adds nothing beyond a shorter prose summary and one new simulated (not measured) illustrative figure; every technical claim it makes is qualified by a forward reference to "our main publication."

### Hardware, wall-clock, and loss-function specifics — what is and is not in this paper

There are none. No hardware is named (no GPU model, no SLM part number, no pixel pitch), no iteration count or convergence criterion is given, and the loss function is described only in one plain-English sentence with no equation. Anything more specific attributed to "the Akşit/Walton metameric varifocal method" — the Freeman-and-Simoncelli-style steerable-pyramid statistics, the pooling-region growth law with eccentricity, the 90 s / 4 s wall-clock figures — must be sourced to arXiv:2110.01981 (which is in `docs/papers/` as `2110.01981.pdf` and was read as part of the earlier survey), not to this document.

### Compute savings vs. quality gains from foveation

This paper offers no evidence either way. It contains not a single number characterizing runtime, iteration count, or computational cost, for either a foveated or non-foveated configuration; the entire document is qualitative. It therefore cannot be cited as a paper that measured a compute saving from foveating a CGH pipeline, but it equally cannot be read as evidence *against* the possibility — it simply never engages with the question at the level of hardware cost. **Our earlier survey's central negative finding — that no published CGH paper reports a measured compute or FLOP saving attributable to foveation itself — stands unmodified by this document.**

### What this changes for HoloPixel

- Nothing changes about the technical understanding of the Akşit/Walton metameric method; it should continue to be cited via arXiv:2110.01981, with this SPIE paper treated purely as a secondary/duplicate reference, not an independent data point.
- This closes out the one paywalled citation in the foveated-holography survey that carried any risk of hiding an unreported number (compute savings, a different loss form, a different hardware target) — having now read it in full, we can say with certainty there was nothing to recover from it.
- No action item for the HoloPixel design falls out of this paper specifically; all actionable content from this research lineage (warm-starting as a transplantable speed trick, eccentricity-weighted loss as a "free" quality lever, both already logged in `research_foveated_holography.md`) originates in arXiv:2110.01981 and arXiv:2108.06192, not here.
