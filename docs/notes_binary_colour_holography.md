# Binary (on/off) hogels and colour holography — literature note

Scope: literature search for "black-and-white, on-off (binary) hogels that can
nonetheless produce colour holograms", done for the holoPixel near-eye
holographic display project. Compared against our own numbers: pixel pitch
1-2.7 um, hogel ~10.6 um across (4-10 pixels/axis, i.e. 16-100 pixels total
per hogel), wavelengths 450/532/632 nm, each pixel only has to spread light
over roughly +-6 to +-14 degrees (spherical panel centred ~20 mm from the eye).

PDFs are in `docs/papers/` (gitignored), converted to PNG with `pdftoppm` and
read page by page.

## 0. Likely candidate

The paper the user is most likely remembering is:

**Goorden, Bertolotti & Mosk, "Superpixel-based spatial amplitude and phase
modulation using a digital micromirror device", Opt. Express 22, 17999
(2014); arXiv:1405.3893.**

It is the seminal, widely-cited demonstration that a purely binary (on/off)
digital micromirror device (DMD) can synthesise an *arbitrary complex*
(amplitude-and-phase) value per resolution cell, by grouping n x n binary
mirrors into a "superpixel" and spatially filtering in a Fourier plane. That
is exactly the striking, quotable fact that matches "black-and-white pixels
making an arbitrary — hence colour-capable — hologram". However: **the 2014
paper itself is monochromatic** (633 nm HeNe laser only) and never discusses
colour. Colour is bolted on afterwards, in separate papers, by cycling the
whole binary pattern through R/G/B illumination in time (see section 5). If
the user's memory includes an explicit colour demonstration on the *same*
device, they may be thinking of one of the follow-on DMD colour-CGH papers
(section 5) rather than the original 2014 paper, or conflating the two.

## 1. Detour-phase holograms (Lohmann/Brown-Lohmann, Lee holograms)

Classic references (pre-arXiv, not downloaded as PDFs here, cited via DOI):

- B. R. Brown & A. W. Lohmann, "Complex spatial filtering with binary
  masks", Appl. Opt. 5(6), 967-969 (1966). DOI: 10.1364/AO.5.000967.
- A. W. Lohmann & D. P. Paris, "Binary Fraunhofer holograms, generated by
  computer", Appl. Opt. 6(10), 1739-1748 (1967). DOI: 10.1364/AO.6.001739.
  This is the paper that introduces detour phase proper: a binary
  (open/closed) aperture inside each cell, whose *position* within the cell
  encodes phase and whose *size* encodes amplitude.
- W.-H. Lee, "Sampled Fourier transform hologram generated by computer",
  Appl. Opt. 9(3), 639-643 (1970).
- W.-H. Lee, "Binary computer-generated holograms", Appl. Opt. 18(21),
  3661-3669 (1979). The "Lee hologram" family (parallel and orthogonal
  variants) is the direct binary-amplitude descendant of detour phase and is
  the standard reference method against which every modern binary-DMD paper
  benchmarks itself (see section 2).

How colour is obtained: not addressed in the originals (interferometric /
single-wavelength optical processing context, 1960s-1970s). Modern
metasurface revivals add colour in two different ways:

- M. Khorasaninejad, A. Ambrosio, P. Kanhaiya & F. Capasso, "Dispersion-
  compensated meta-holograms based on detour phase", arXiv:1601.01415.
  Achieves *achromatic* (wavelength-independent) detour-phase operation from
  750-1400 nm by engineering the meta-atoms' own dispersion to cancel the
  detour-phase grating's native wavelength dependence; efficiency up to 75%
  in the 1000-1400 nm band (near-IR, dielectric metasurface, not amplitude
  binary in the DMD sense — the underlying phase mask is binary in the
  classic detour-phase sense, aperture position/absence, but implemented as
  nanostructures rather than raw on/off mirror pixels).
- Z.-L. Deng et al., "Full-color complex-amplitude vectorial holograms based
  on multi-freedom metasurfaces", arXiv:1912.11184. Combines detour phase
  with geometric (Pancharatnam-Berry) phase to get complex-amplitude control
  at several discrete wavelengths from *the same* nanostructure layout,
  demonstrating full-colour meta-holograms in the visible. Efficiency and
  crosstalk are metasurface-fabrication-dependent, not quantified as a
  simple percentage in the abstract-level material reviewed here.

Pixel cost / efficiency: for the *simple* binary off-axis hologram
(equivalent to raw detour phase without further tricks), one grating period
encodes one complex sample, at a hard efficiency ceiling of 6.25% into the
useful diffraction order (see section 2, Eq. 5-6 in the Gutiérrez-Cuevas &
Popoff review). Optimised "parallel Lee" duty-cycle encoding raises this to
~10.1% (same review).

Demonstrated: yes (hardware, since the 1960s; metasurface revivals also
hardware-demonstrated).

Fits in our hogel: **the metasurface revivals are not hogel-tileable binary
amplitude pixel arrays** (they are engineered nanostructures, not a simple
on/off shutter grid at our 1-2.7 um pitch), so they are only loosely
"adjacent". The classic binary detour-phase/Lee scheme, mapped onto our
pixel grid, needs one full grating period (several pixels) per complex
sample — see section 2 for the quantitative version of this trade-off, which
applies equally here.

## 2. The superpixel method and its generalisations

- S. A. Goorden, J. Bertolotti & A. P. Mosk, "Superpixel-based spatial
  amplitude and phase modulation using a digital micromirror device",
  arXiv:1405.3893 (Opt. Express 22, 17999, 2014). **Primary candidate, see
  section 0.**
- R. Gutiérrez-Cuevas & S. M. Popoff, "Binary holograms for shaping light
  with digital micromirror devices", arXiv:2311.16685 (2023). A didactic,
  quantitative review that puts Lee holograms, "Haskell" holograms, and the
  superpixel method side by side with the *same* metric (field/intensity
  correlation to target, and diffraction efficiency), which is exactly the
  exchange-rate data we need.

Mechanism: n x n (or more generally px x py, with py = px^2 to fully lift a
phase degeneracy) binary DMD pixels are imaged with a deliberate off-axis
(4f) geometry and Fourier-filtered so that the n^2 (or px*py) individual
pixel responses land at *different, evenly-spaced phases* around the unit
circle in the target plane. Summing any subset of "on" pixels inside the
superpixel produces a vector sum landing anywhere inside a disk in the
complex plane — i.e. one superpixel = one arbitrary (quantised) complex
sample.

Exact exchange rate (from both papers, consistent with each other):

- n=3 isotropic superpixel: 9 binary pixels -> 1 complex sample, 3^2=343 (in
  the review's px x px^2 formulation, tilted, ~ px * k pixels, with
  correlation to target CE/CI up to 99.8/99.9% at px=3, i.e. 3*9=27 pixels)
  reachable field values, roughly uniformly spread over a disk.
- n=4 isotropic superpixel (used for the 2014 paper's main hardware
  demonstration): 16 binary pixels -> 1 complex sample, 6561 reachable field
  values, measured fidelity F=0.98 on a fully-independent-amplitude-and-phase
  test field, F=0.99993 on an LG10 mode.
  In the review's tilted px x px^2 formulation with px=4: 4*16=64 pixels ->
  1 complex sample, CE/CI ~99.7/99.8%.
- The Table I data in the review (px = 3,4,5,6) shows diffraction efficiency
  eta_d of the superpixel/Haskell method sitting at 5.2-10.1% depending on
  px and its parity, essentially matching plain Lee holography's efficiency
  — the superpixel method buys much better fidelity per pixel spent, not
  higher efficiency.

Efficiency, measured (Goorden 2014 hardware): theoretical modulation
efficiency 10.3% (turning on exactly half the superpixel's pixels); measured
modulation efficiency 7%; **total measured system efficiency 4%** once the
DMD's own 60% zero-order mirror efficiency is folded in. This 4% number is
the one to compare against a phase-only modulator.

Colour: **not addressed in either paper.** Both are single-wavelength
demonstrations (633 nm). Wavelength dispersion of the spatial filter's
position/size means the whole superpixel calibration (aperture position,
lookup table) is wavelength-specific; the 2014 paper notes the method
tolerates |delta_lambda/lambda| up to ~10% before the target field starts
missing the spatial filter, and a phase gradient of order n*lambda/delta_lambda
pixels appears before that limit is reached. In practice this means a
different superpixel calibration/lookup table per colour channel, used
time-sequentially (see section 5) — not a single pattern that is
simultaneously correct at all three colours.

Demonstrated: yes, hardware (DMD), monochromatic only.

Fits in our hogel: **this is the central quantitative result of this
search.** Our hogel is only 4-10 pixels across per axis, i.e. 16-100 pixels
total. Even the *cheapest* usable superpixel (n=3, 9 pixels) spent on ONE
arbitrary complex sample means a 4-pixel-wide hogel can fit at most one such
sample per axis (so effectively one controllable direction for the whole
hogel), and a 10-pixel-wide hogel can fit at most about 3x3 = 9 independent
samples using its full budget, leaving no margin for anything else. Compare
this to an 8-level (or continuous) phase-only modulator, where *every
individual pixel* is already one directly-usable complex-ish sample (unit
amplitude, one of 8 phases) — a 10x10-pixel hogel gives up to 100 usable
phase samples "for free", not 9. **The superpixel method does not fit our
hogel geometry**: it would consume most or all of a hogel's pixel budget to
emit a single controllable ray direction, defeating the purpose of a hogel
that is supposed to emit a whole bundle of rays across +-6 to +-14 degrees.

## 3. Binary amplitude CGH on DMDs generally (beyond superpixel)

- T. Shimobaba et al., "Simple complex amplitude encoding of a phase-only
  hologram using binarized amplitude", arXiv:1909.08177 (2019). A
  post-processing trick: binarise the *amplitude* term used to correct a
  phase-only hologram, recorded on top of the phase-only hologram plane.
  Different problem from ours (it is about improving phase-only SLM image
  quality, not building a binary-only display) but confirms the general
  motif: binary amplitude is enough to carry the missing amplitude
  information, at the cost of extra pixels/lines in the hologram.
- A. Ghosh et al. (Politecnico di Milano / EssilorLuxottica "Smart Eyewear
  Lab"), "Iterative approach for high-quality binary intensity hologram
  generation in augmented reality applications", arXiv:2604.11181 (2026).
  Directly AR-motivated. Applies the binarisation constraint *inside* a
  Gerchberg-Saxton-like loop (ITR) instead of as a final step, on an
  **off-axis, whole-panel** binary amplitude hologram (not hogel-tiled).
  Reports large *relative* quality gains over random-superposition (RS) and
  standard GS binarisation: e.g. PSNR 27.3 dB / contrast 9.17 (ITR) vs 24.0
  dB / 3.98 (GS) vs 22.6 dB / 3.88 (RS) on a single-plane test object; also
  demonstrated on 3D point-cloud content. Explicitly notes that binary
  intensity holograms are "inevitably less efficient than phase holograms in
  terms of light power" but argues they matter because **emerging
  sub-wavelength-pixel, wide-field-of-view modulator technologies are, for
  now, limited to binary intensity modulation** — a directly relevant
  argument for our 1-2.7 um pixel pitch regime. No colour is addressed
  (monochrome only, grayscale test images).
- The Gutiérrez-Cuevas & Popoff review (section 2) also covers this ground
  generally and is the best single source for the pixels-per-sample /
  efficiency / fidelity trade-off curve.

Demonstrated: yes, hardware (DMD), monochrome.

Fits in our hogel: **architecturally mismatched, not just a pixel-count
problem.** These are *global, whole-panel* Fourier holograms: the entire
DMD (thousands x thousands of pixels) acts as a single grating reconstructing
one target image at a projection/eyebox plane. There is no natural way to
decompose this into independent, small (4-10 pixel) hogels each responsible
for its own local ray bundle — a 5-10 pixel-wide binary grating patch simply
does not have the bandwidth to shape an arbitrary local wavefront; it can
only produce a couple of fixed, widely-spaced diffraction orders. This is a
different display architecture from hogel-tiled light-field displays.

## 4. Wavelength-multiplexed / dispersion-engineered binary holograms

This line turned out to be thin for genuinely *binary, on/off* pixel arrays;
essentially all real examples live in the metasurface world, where "binary"
usually means "structure present at this discrete phase level" rather than a
literal two-state shutter:

- C. Huang et al. (as indexed under "Wavelength de-multiplexing metasurface
  hologram"), Sci. Rep. 6, 35657 (2016), DOI: 10.1038/srep35657. Different
  images reconstructed at two THz frequencies (0.50 and 0.63 THz) from one
  metasurface. Confirms the *principle* (wavelength/angle-dependent
  diffraction lets one static pattern serve multiple channels) but is a THz
  antenna array, not a visible-light binary amplitude pixel grid, and not
  hogel-scale.
- Khorasaninejad et al. arXiv:1601.01415 (section 1) goes the *opposite*
  direction: engineering dispersion away (achromatic detour phase) rather
  than exploiting it for multiplexing.
- Deng et al. arXiv:1912.11184 (section 1) uses frequency-dependent detour
  phase deliberately, combined with geometric phase, to reach full colour
  from one design strategy — the closest visible-light match to "one binary
  pattern, several colours", but again a fabricated dielectric/plasmonic
  metasurface with many phase levels per meta-atom, not a 1-2.7 um pitch
  on/off shutter array.

No candidate here is both (a) genuinely binary/on-off at the pixel level and
(b) a visible-light, hogel-scale demonstration. This line is real but
adjacent, not central, to what the user is remembering.

Fits in our hogel: no direct answer possible — none of these are hogel-
tiled binary pixel arrays; they are single continuous metasurface holograms
covering the whole aperture, fabricated with far more degrees of freedom per
unit cell (structure shape, orientation, size) than a plain on/off shutter.

## 5. Time-sequential colour for binary-amplitude DMD holography

This is, on the evidence gathered, **the actual practical route by which
"binary on/off pixels" papers get to colour**, and is likely what a memory of
"binary hogels producing colour holograms" is really describing once colour
is involved at all:

- S. Yoshida, "High-speed full-color computer-generated holography using a
  digital micromirror device and fiber-coupled RGB laser diode",
  arXiv:2310.10093 (2023). Cycles the *entire* binary hologram through
  red/green/blue illumination in time (time-division multiplexing), relying
  on the DMD's high switching speed (~100x LC-SLM) to keep the effective
  full-colour frame rate usable despite the inherent 1/3 duty-cycle penalty
  of splitting time three ways. Compensates DMD wavelength dispersion (the
  DMD acts as a blazed grating, pixel pitch 7.56 um in this setup, diffraction
  angle is wavelength-dependent) by superimposing a corrective plane-wave
  phase ramp per colour rather than re-steering the illumination angle.
  Demonstrated hologram switching >3000 fps and full-colour rotating-object
  video reconstruction at 72 fps (camera-limited), hardware, real RGB laser
  diodes. No colour crosstalk within a frame (each frame is single-
  wavelength; crosstalk would only appear as motion/temporal artefacts
  across frames, not spectral bleed).
- Related "Dynamic full-color digital holographic 3D display on single DMD"
  (found via search, not separately downloaded) combines a superpixel-style
  complex-amplitude method with this same time-division colour approach —
  i.e., section 2's superpixel method plus section 5's time multiplexing is
  the natural way the two candidate lines the user might be remembering
  would actually be combined into a colour display.

How colour is obtained: strictly time-sequential — one binary pattern per
colour, cycled fast enough that the eye/camera integrates the three into one
colour frame. Not simultaneous, not from a single static pattern.

Cost: no additional pixels spent per complex sample (that cost is whatever
the underlying monochrome CGH method needs, sections 1-3); the cost is
paid in *time* (frame rate divided by the number of colour channels, here
~3x) and in requiring three synchronised laser sources.

Efficiency: inherits whatever the underlying monochrome binary CGH method's
efficiency is (4-10% range, sections 1-3); not itself a source of additional
loss beyond the duty-cycle division.

Demonstrated: yes, hardware, full-colour video.

Fits in our hogel: **time-division colour is orthogonal to the hogel pixel-
budget problem** — it does not change how many pixels a hogel needs per
complex sample, it only affects overall frame rate/architecture. It could in
principle be layered on top of any of the spatial encoding schemes above,
but since none of those fit our hogel geometry (sections 1-3), layering
time-division colour on top does not rescue them. It would, however, be a
perfectly viable way to add colour if we ever did move to a binary spatial
modulator with a compatible pixel budget.

## 6. Halftoning / error diffusion

Mostly an image-processing technique for turning grayscale intensity into
1-bit patterns (dot diffusion, blue-noise error diffusion, learned
halftoning), used as the binarisation step *inside* schemes from section 3
rather than a distinct optical architecture. Ghosh et al.'s ITR method
(section 3) is essentially an error-diffusion-like idea (binarise inside the
iteration loop rather than after) applied specifically to CGH. No
colour-specific literature of note was found beyond standard three-channel
(CMY/RGB) halftone printing, which is not optically relevant to a
holographic display. Not treated as a separate row in the comparison table
below; folded into section 3.

## Verdict

Every scheme found that is genuinely (a) binary/on-off at the pixel level
and (b) meant to synthesise an arbitrary directional/complex value, needs
somewhere between about 8 and 64+ binary pixels to fake one such value, at
4-10% optical efficiency. Our hogel has only 16-100 pixels *total*. Spending
even the cheapest version of this trick (9 pixels/sample) leaves a 4-pixel
hogel able to produce one usable direction and a 10-pixel hogel able to
produce roughly nine — nowhere near enough independent ray directions to
synthesise a useful bundle across +-6 to +-14 degrees, and at a fraction of
the efficiency of the 8-level phase-change stack this would replace, where
every pixel is already a usable, nearly-full-amplitude sample. The
whole-panel binary CGH literature (section 3) does not even attempt to
decompose into small hogels, so it is architecturally the wrong shape for
our display regardless of pixel budget. Colour, when it appears at all in
this literature, is added by time-multiplexing three monochrome binary
patterns (section 5), which does not change this conclusion — it neither
helps nor hurts the pixel-budget problem, it only costs frame rate.

Net: a plain on/off shutter cannot be dropped in to replace the eight-level
phase-change modulator at our hogel size. The complexity that stack buys
(mirror stack, AR coat, per-colour tuning, 8-level LUT) is doing real work
that a binary shutter cannot do inside a 4-10 pixel hogel at any efficiency
worth having.
